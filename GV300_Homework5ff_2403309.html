Cronbach’s alpha for the Q37 battery is about 0.92, which is well above the usual 0.7 threshold. This means the items are quite internally consistent — people who trust one institution tend to trust others too, and vice versa. Based on this I would feel fairly comfortable combining them into a single “institutional trust” scale. One thing to note is that the battery includes quite different institutions (NHS, social media, courts, etc.) so it is possible that a factor analysis might reveal sub-dimensions, but the overall internal consistency is strong.

Conceptual question: A high alpha does not prove that a scale is valid. Alpha only tells us about internal consistency — whether the items hang together. Validity is about whether we are actually measuring the thing we think we are measuring. For example, all 12 items could be picking up on a general tendency to give high or low ratings (acquiescence bias) rather than genuine trust. To assess validity we would need to look at things like whether the scale correlates with outcomes we would expect it to (criterion validity) or whether the items actually map onto a coherent construct of “institutional trust” (construct validity).

I would choose to present the average score in an article. The average score remains in the original 0-10 response scale, which is much easier read, a mean of 4.2 will at once tell you something about the degree of trust, whereas a total of 50 will tell you more, without knowing how many items are summed up. Reliability threat: One threat to reliability in this battery is that the items cover very different types of institutions — social media and the NHS, for example, are quite different things. Respondents might interpret “trust” differently depending on the institution, introducing noise and reducing the consistency of the scale across measurement occasions.

Validity threat: A key validity concern is social desirability bias or acquiescence bias. Some respondents might tend to give moderate or high scores across the board without carefully thinking about each institution, meaning the scale captures response style rather than genuine trust.

Potential source of bias: Respondents who are more politically engaged or informed might anchor their answers to recent news events (like a political scandal or an NHS crisis), whereas less informed respondents might give more generic middle-of-the-road answers. This means the scale could be measuring different things for different groups of people.
